Three Roles Walked In. One Walked Out.

Data Leadership · Ankush Arya · May 29, 2026

On BA, developer, and QA roles, the compression AI is driving, and why the thinking that made those roles matter has not gone anywhere

Something quietly broke in the software industry last year. Most people did not notice because it did not look like a breaking. It looked like a product update.

The companies that noticed first were not startups. They were the large, slow-moving institutions that take years to change direction and decades to admit they were wrong. Banks. Technology giants. The kind of organisations that once hired in cohorts of hundreds and called it a pipeline.

They started trimming.

And the interesting part is not the number of roles lost. It is which roles. Because when you look closely, a pattern emerges. The jobs disappearing fastest are not the ones that were poorly defined. They are the ones that existed specifically to translate between other jobs. The analyst who sat between the business and the developer. The tester who sat between the developer and production. The coordinator who managed the handover so nothing got lost in the gap.

Those gaps are closing. And something is closing them.

Across the technology and financial services sectors, large organisations have been reducing headcount in software and operations functions. The stated rationale, in earnings calls, internal communications, and public announcements, has increasingly pointed toward AI-driven productivity gains and reallocation of resource toward AI capability. The scale varies. The direction does not.

If that feels distant, it should not. Because the same tools making those decisions possible are sitting in your browser right now.

I Did Not Expect It Either

I use multiple AI tools in my own work. And I will be honest: I resisted updating my mental model for longer than I should have.

Then I watched a complete data pipeline, a FastAPI endpoint, meaningful test coverage, and deployment configuration come together inside a single working session. Context held across the whole conversation. The output was not perfect. But it was not the parlour trick I had been quietly assuming it was.

What changed my mind was not the output itself. It was realising that what I was watching was not a shortcut. It was a compression. Something that previously required three people, two handover documents, and a week of back and forth had collapsed into a single focused session.

That is not a small thing. That is a structural shift in how software gets built.

Why the Three Roles Existed in the First Place

Here is what the efficiency narrative keeps getting wrong.

The BA, the developer, and the QA engineer did not exist because organisations were inefficient. They existed because the work genuinely required three different ways of thinking. Not three different skill sets sitting in the same cognitive lane. Three fundamentally distinct modes of engaging with a problem.

A BA is trained to hold ambiguity without collapsing it too early. To sit with a messy, half-formed requirement and resist the pull toward premature solutioning. Their most valuable contribution often happens before a single line of code is written, in the moment they push back and say: I do not think this is the actual problem.

A developer is trained to reason in constraints. To think about what happens at scale, under pressure, at three in the morning when something breaks in production and the logs are not telling the full story. Good developers do not just write code that works today. They write code that fails gracefully tomorrow.

A QA engineer is trained to think adversarially. To find the scenario nobody wrote down, the edge case that lived in the gap between what the specification said and what the user actually does. They are the ones who ask: what happens if someone does exactly what we told them not to do.

Those are not job titles. They are cognitive modes. And they matter now more than ever.

What Is Actually Being Compressed

What AI tooling is reducing is not those cognitive modes. It is the mechanical friction between them.

The ticket that took two days to write and one meeting to clarify. The specification document that captured sixty percent of the requirement and left the rest to interpretation. The handover where half the context lived in someone's head and never made it into writing. That friction — the translation layer between thinking and building — is being compressed dramatically.

And what that means in practice is that a single capable professional with genuine fluency in these tools can now move between those cognitive modes within a single working session. Ask the BA question before writing the prompt. Build and test within the same conversation. Catch the adversarial edge case before it becomes a production incident.

That is not one person doing three jobs badly. That is the feedback loop getting faster.

The assumption that caught a bad requirement before it became bad architecture. The test that ran before the pull request was opened. The question that saved three weeks of the wrong work.

That changes what seniority means. Not the ability to occupy a lane with deep expertise and defend it. The ability to move between lanes fluently and know which one the work is actually in at any given moment.

What This Asks of You

The professionals navigating this well are not the loudest voices in either direction.

Not the ones who declared in 2023 that AI would change everything and have been waiting for the revolution to arrive on schedule. Not the ones quietly hoping it turns out to be a cycle, that things settle back to how they were and the job titles return to what they used to mean.

They are the ones who built genuine fluency with the tools without losing sight of what the tools cannot do. They use AI for the mechanical translation. They stay present for the judgement. They understand that the tool can compress the gap between modes but it cannot supply the modes themselves.

The BA instinct that says we are solving the wrong problem. The developer instinct that says this will not hold under real load. The QA instinct that says someone will absolutely do the thing we assumed they would not. Those still require a person who has spent enough time in the work to know what the work is actually asking.

The roles may be merging. The thinking has not gone anywhere.

The most valuable professionals in this next period will not be the ones who picked a side between old and new. They will be the ones who understand when each earns its place. Who can move between ambiguity, implementation, and adversarial thinking within a single session and know which one the moment is calling for.

That is not a job title. It is a capability. And right now, it is your advantage.
